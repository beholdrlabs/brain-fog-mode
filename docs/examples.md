*The real website address and links have been removed to anonymize this example. The recorded responses are otherwise unchanged.*

**Prompt:**

We have an online jewelry shop what should we do to increase sales [website removed]

### Without Brain Fog Mode

**I’d start by making buyers more confident about ordering, then bring in more people specifically looking for Masonic jewelry.** Your specialty gives you a much clearer marketing direction than advertising broadly to jewelry shoppers.

I reviewed your homepage, one ring product page, FAQ, refund policy and custom-design page. I haven’t seen your sales analytics or tested checkout, so these are priorities to investigate rather than proven causes of lost sales.

1. **Fix conflicting shipping and availability information first.** Your homepage promotes free worldwide shipping, but your FAQ says shipping costs vary and are calculated at checkout. The amethyst ring page says “In stock,” while its description says items are made to order and take approximately 4–5 weeks to arrive. Make the actual shipping offer and production time consistent everywhere. Put expected delivery, duties and a ring-sizing link directly beside the purchase button.

2. **Bring your strongest reasons to buy closer to the decision.** Your site already shows an Etsy review score, handmade production and a distinctive founder story. Put relevant customer reviews, a short workshop video, metal specifications and the warranty near your bestselling products. Add photos showing each piece worn on a hand or body, with dimensions, so customers can judge its size. Make the return-policy summary easy to see: standard catalogue pieces have a 14-day return window; personalized pieces have different conditions.

3. **Make the homepage’s main message more specific.** Try a headline such as “Handcrafted Masonic Jewelry in Solid Silver and Gold,” supported by one sentence about your craftsmanship and personalization. Give visitors clear routes to “Shop Masonic Rings,” “Shop by Rite” and “Commission a Piece.” You already organize collections by rite; build on that with curated gift selections for degree milestones, Past Masters and lodge occasions. Test this messaging against the current page.

4. **Treat custom commissions as a separate sales opportunity.** Your custom-design page explains the process and starting price well. Add a prominent “Discuss your commission” button and a short inquiry form asking for the symbol, metal, budget and occasion/date. Present previous commissions as examples with “Request something similar,” especially where a finished commission currently appears sold out. Track qualified inquiries and how many become paid orders.

5. **Start with focused discovery and email marketing.** Check whether your products are eligible and active in [Google’s free product listings](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/13889434?hl=en-GB). Improve collection pages around specific searches such as “sterling silver Masonic rings” and “Past Master gifts,” using accurate material and symbol descriptions. Publish short videos showing a design becoming a finished piece, linking to that product. Review your email setup: Shopify supports [abandoned checkout, cart and browse automations](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/promoting-marketing/create-marketing/shopify-messaging/marketing-automations/create). Use eligible customer contacts and start with helpful reminders about sizing, delivery and craftsmanship.

**For the next 30 days:** fix the information conflicts, improve your five bestselling product pages, strengthen the commission inquiry route, and check free listings and email recovery. Then test a small, tightly targeted advertising campaign if your margins support it.

Measure weekly sessions, purchase conversion rate, average order value, acquisition cost and profit after marketing. For commissions, measure inquiries separately. Those numbers will tell you whether your next investment should go toward attracting visitors or helping existing visitors buy.

What are your monthly website visits, orders, average order value and main customer countries? Those four figures would make the next recommendations much more precise.

<details>
<summary>White chocolate cookies</summary>

**Prompt:**

Help me with a recipe, I want to make white chocolate cookies.

### Without Brain Fog Mode

Here’s a simple recipe for **soft white chocolate cookies**, with lightly crisp edges. Makes about 16 cookies.

**Ingredients**

- 115 g unsalted butter, softened
- 100 g light brown sugar
- 50 g white sugar
- 1 large egg
- 1 tsp vanilla extract
- 190 g plain flour
- ½ tsp baking soda
- ½ tsp salt
- 150 g white chocolate, chopped or chips

**Method**

1. Beat the butter and both sugars until creamy. Mix in the egg and vanilla.
2. In another bowl, combine the flour, baking soda, and salt. Stir into the butter mixture just until combined, then fold in the chocolate.
3. Chill the dough for 30 minutes. Heat the oven to **180°C conventional / 160°C fan** and line a baking tray with baking paper.
4. Scoop into roughly 2-tablespoon portions, leaving about 5 cm between them. Bake in batches for **10–12 minutes**, until the edges are lightly golden and the centres still look soft.
5. Leave on the tray for 10 minutes, then transfer to a rack.

For softer cookies, take them out before the centres look fully baked—they firm up as they cool. A little flaky salt on top balances the sweetness.

---

### With Brain Fog Mode

**Make soft white chocolate cookies.** This batch makes about 16 cookies.

**Ingredients**

- 115 g unsalted butter, softened
- 100 g light brown sugar
- 50 g white sugar
- 1 large egg
- 1 tsp vanilla extract
- 180 g plain flour
- ½ tsp baking soda
- ¼ tsp salt
- 150 g white chocolate chips or chopped white chocolate

**Method**

1. Beat the butter and both sugars until creamy. Mix in the egg and vanilla.
2. Stir together the flour, baking soda, and salt. Mix into the butter mixture just until combined, then fold in the chocolate.
3. Chill the dough for 30 minutes.
4. Heat the oven to **175°C conventional / 160°C fan**. Line two baking trays with baking paper.
5. Divide into 16 balls and place about 5 cm apart. Bake one tray at a time for **10–12 minutes**, until the edges are lightly golden and the centers still look soft.
6. Leave on the tray for 10 minutes, then move to a rack to cool.

**Takeaway:** A complete recipe, with the ingredients and steps kept easy to scan.

<summary>Studying AWS systems engineering</summary>

**Prompt:**

Help me study systems engineering, AWS platform focused. I don't know where to begin.

### Without Brain Fog Mode

Start with **how a small system works**, then learn the AWS services that provide each part. That will give you a stronger foundation than memorizing service names.

I’d use this four-week starting plan, with roughly 30–45 minutes a day:

| Week | Learn | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Linux basics, processes, files, HTTP, DNS, and networking | Follow what happens when a browser requests a page |
| 2 | AWS regions, availability zones, IAM, compute, and storage | Draw a system using EC2, S3, and a database; explain each component |
| 3 | VPCs, security groups, monitoring, and permissions | Deploy a small app in a learning environment and inspect its logs |
| 4 | Reliability, backups, automation, and cost | Describe how your app handles failure, restores data, and gets updated |

For AWS fundamentals, use the official [Cloud Essentials learning path](https://aws.amazon.com/training/learn-about/cloud-practitioner/), which covers core services, security, and pricing. Once you have a small working system, use the [AWS Well-Architected Framework](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/welcome.html) to assess its reliability, security, and operating choices.

For any personal AWS labs, check pricing, set a budget alert, and remove resources when finished. Budget alerts are notifications, not a spending cap.

**Today:** Spend 30 minutes on the first Cloud Essentials lesson. Write down what a server does, what a network does, and what storage does. These are the building blocks you’ll keep returning to.

Have you used Linux or written any code before, and are you studying for a job, a certification, or a project?

---

### With Brain Fog Mode

**Begin with one small lesson: how a web request moves through an AWS system.** I'll assume you're starting with the basics; say `more advanced` if that is too easy.

Use this simplified path:

**Visitor → load balancer → web server → database**

The load balancer distributes requests; the web server runs the application; the database stores its data. AWS's [web-and-database example, Overview and Security sections](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/en_en/vpc/latest/userguide/vpc-example-web-database-servers.html) shows that flow and how access between the parts is restricted.

**Next:** On paper, draw those four boxes and add one sentence describing each box's job. Keep this first exercise on paper, so it needs no AWS account or paid resources.

**Takeaway:** One exercise to start with instead of a four-week roadmap.

<details>
<summary>Using AI agents efficiently</summary>

**Prompt:**

How can I use my AI agents more efficiently? I spent a lot of tokens last week.

### Without Brain Fog Mode

Start by finding which tasks consumed the tokens. Long conversations, repeated investigation, retry loops, and overlapping agents are useful places to check, but your usage data should determine what you change.

For the next week, try these adjustments:

- **Give each task a clear finish line.** State the desired result, relevant files or sources, constraints, and how success should be checked.
- **Keep context focused.** Supply the relevant excerpt or file paths instead of entire logs or repository dumps. Start a fresh session for unrelated work; carry over a short handoff with decisions and remaining work.
- **Use fewer agents by default.** Delegate when subtasks are independent and each worker has a distinct output. Multiple agents investigating the same problem can duplicate reading and reasoning.
- **Match model effort to the task.** Try a smaller model or lower reasoning effort for straightforward work, and escalate when it fails your quality checks. OpenAI’s [cost guidance](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/cost-optimization) recommends reducing unnecessary tokens and selecting appropriately sized models.
- **Put boundaries on exploration.** Ask an agent to report a blocker after a few failed attempts instead of continuing an unproductive loop.
- **Measure cost per completed task.** Track retries and whether the result was usable. Fewer tokens are only helpful if the task still gets done.

A reusable task prompt:

Complete [specific outcome]. Relevant context: [files or excerpts]. Keep changes within [scope]. Verify with [check]. Stop when that passes. If blocked after three attempts, report the evidence and the missing decision. Return a concise result.

If you use an API, also check [prompt caching](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-caching) for repeated context. Caching can reduce input cost, but it does not eliminate the tokens in that context.

Which agent tools are you using, and was last week’s usage mostly coding, research, or automated workflows?

---

### With Brain Fog Mode

**Start by reducing repeated context in one recurring task.** Give the agent a clear outcome, a stopping condition, and only the relevant files or excerpts. Preserve decisions and unfinished work in a short handoff when you start a fresh conversation.

This follows [Anthropic's context guidance](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents), which recommends focused context and summaries that retain critical details. I haven't seen your usage, so I don't know whether repeated context caused last week's token use.

**Next:** Try this on one familiar task and compare its total tokens and result quality with a similar previous run.

**Takeaway:** One reversible trial, with token use and result quality checked together.
